Overview
Executive Summary

Al Saber Cars operates a showroom whose inventory spans the most demanding brands in the automotive world — Rolls-Royce, Ferrari, Lamborghini, Bentley — alongside a strong everyday range. The website carrying that inventory does not perform at the level of the cars, in any of the three dimensions that matter: how fast it loads, how premium it feels, and whether Google can find it.

The performance numbers are the most urgent. Google PageSpeed measured the homepage at 55/100 on mobile and 54/100 on desktop — the desktop score being equally poor proves this is not a network issue but a construction issue. The site downloads 34 MB, and its main image takes 57.5 seconds to render on a standard mobile connection — twenty-three times over Google's 2.5-second standard — and still 9.2 seconds on a fast desktop line. A first-time mobile visitor sees a blank screen for over nine seconds. For a clientele that decides in seconds whether a brand feels like their world, the site loses a large share of visitors before showing a single car.

Search visibility is structurally absent rather than weak: the meta description is an empty string, no page carries an H1 heading, there is no sitemap or robots.txt, 125 of 126 images have no alt text, and no structured data exists — meaning no car, no price, and no brand from the inventory can surface as a rich result. The site exists only in Arabic, making it invisible to English-language luxury buyers in Riyadh and Jeddah. Separately, the customer journey has conversion dead-ends at its most valuable moment: the car detail page offers a single watermarked photo, a two-word description, and no WhatsApp, call, or viewing-booking action. This audit documents 30 issues across 8 categories; each one is specific, verifiable, and fixable.

Issue Findings
Website Issues — 30 Findings
Performance & Delivery — Findings 1–8
1
The Homepage Downloads 34 MB — 17× the Size of an Average Web Page
PageSpeed measured the total network payload at 34,252 KiB and flagged it as an "enormous network payload." The median web page is roughly 2 MB. Every first-time visitor — including every person clicking an Instagram ad or a shared WhatsApp link — must download the equivalent of a short video before the page completes. On mobile data, that is a real cost in both time and dirhams, paid at the exact moment the site should be making a first impression.
2
The First Car a Visitor Sees Takes 57.5 Seconds to Render
Largest Contentful Paint — the moment the main hero image appears — measured 57.5 seconds on Google's standard mobile test conditions, against a 2.5-second threshold for "good." The hero slider loads multi-megapixel PNG cut-outs through a secondary slider domain, and they are the last thing to arrive. In practice, the visitors this site most needs to impress will never wait to see the Rolls-Royce.
3
Nine Seconds of Blank Screen Before Anything Appears
First Contentful Paint measured 9.4 seconds (threshold: 1.8s) and Speed Index 19.5 seconds (threshold: 3.4s). Industry benchmark data consistently shows the majority of mobile visitors abandon a page that shows nothing within about 3 seconds. The current site loses these visitors before a single pixel of a car is on screen — and they register as zero in any analytics, which is consistent with the "no conversions from the website" experience.
4
Render-Blocking Resources Delay the Page by an Estimated 9 Seconds
PageSpeed identified render-blocking requests with an estimated savings of 8,980 ms — CSS and JavaScript files (Bootstrap, slider libraries, font packs) that must fully download before the browser is allowed to draw anything. This is the direct cause of the blank-screen period in finding 3 and is fixable through standard deferral and critical-CSS techniques without changing how the site looks.
5
Images Are Served Unoptimised — 17 MB of Identified Waste
The "improve image delivery" audit found 17,126 KiB of potential savings. Car photos and hero cut-outs are delivered as full-resolution PNGs with no modern formats (WebP/AVIF), no responsive sizing for mobile screens, and no lazy-loading discipline. For an inventory site whose product is photography, image pipeline is core infrastructure — this is the single largest fixable contributor to the 34 MB payload.
6
No Effective Caching — Returning Visitors Re-download 26 MB
PageSpeed flagged inefficient cache lifetimes on 26,670 KiB of assets. A visitor who saw the site yesterday — or navigates from the homepage to a car page and back — re-downloads nearly the entire payload. Serious buyers visit a dealer's site repeatedly while deciding; the current configuration makes every one of those visits as slow as the first.
7
~900 KiB of Unused and Unminified Code From a Heavy Library Stack
The audit found 311 KiB of unused CSS, 399 KiB of unused JavaScript, and unminified files worth a further 213 KiB. The site loads jQuery, Bootstrap, RevSlider, Slick, Select2, SweetAlert2 and Font Awesome together — a 2015-era stack where each library ships far more code than the site uses. This is a symptom of template construction rather than purpose-built development.
8
Images Declared Without Dimensions
Image elements lack explicit width and height attributes, which forces the browser to recalculate layout as each image arrives. The measured layout shift is currently low (0.015) only because images arrive so late; fixing the speed issues without fixing dimensions would surface visible content-jumping on a faster site.
Design & Brand — Findings 9–13
9
Flagship Cars Are Presented as Cut-Out Images on Grey Shapes, With Cropped Headlines
The hero slider presents the Rolls-Royce Spectre and Ferrari Purosangue as flat PNG cut-outs floating on grey rectangles, with the display headline ("SPECTRE", "FERRARI") partially cut off at the screen edge on both desktop and mobile. These are marques whose manufacturers spend millions of dollars on presentation standards, and their buyers are conditioned by that standard. The gap between how the manufacturer presents the car and how the dealer's website presents it is felt instantly — and it prices the dealership down in the visitor's mind.
10
Luxury and Economy Inventory Share One Undifferentiated Grid
A Mercedes G63 AMG at 465,000 SAR is listed in the same card format, in the same stream, directly beside a 180,000 SAR family SUV. The premium buyer reads this as "trader, not curator" — the digital equivalent of parking the Phantom in a general used-car lot. The value buyer gains nothing from the mixture either. One inventory serving two very different audiences requires two presentation experiences; currently there is one, calibrated to neither.
11
60+ Identical Brand Logo Tiles Dominate the Homepage
The largest single section of the homepage is a uniform grid of more than sixty grey brand tiles — Ferrari next to Isuzu next to Dongfeng, all with equal weight. It communicates breadth at the cost of positioning: the page reads like a parts catalogue or an aggregator rather than a curated house of automobiles. Breadth is a real strength of the business, but it needs hierarchy — the marquee brands presented as a collection, the volume brands as a searchable capability.
12
The Site's Visual Identity Does Not Carry the Al Saber Brand
This audit confirms the concern that prompted it: the site is built on generic template components — stock Bootstrap layouts, default component styling, system-grade typography — with the Al Saber logo placed on top rather than an identity built out of it. Nothing in the colour system, type, or graphic language is ownable or recognisable. A returning visitor would not identify a screenshot of this site as Al Saber without reading the header.
13
Primary Car Photography Is Watermarked Warehouse Shots
Listing and detail images are single photographs taken in the warehouse/showroom with a logo watermark across them. Watermarks signal fear of theft, not pride of presentation, and warehouse backdrops undercut cars that are photographed by their manufacturers in studios. A consistent photography standard — even a simple branded backdrop corner of the showroom — would transform perceived quality at near-zero cost.
Conversion & Customer Journey — Findings 14–19
14
The Car Detail Page — the Decision Moment — Is Nearly Empty
The page where a buyer decides whether to act contains: one watermarked photo, a specification icon row, and a description that is literally two words ("بورش تارجا" on the Porsche Targa GTS page). No photo gallery, no video, no interior shots, no condition report, no story of the car. This is the single most valuable page type on the site and currently its weakest. Everything upstream — ads, social, search — pays to deliver visitors to a page that cannot close.
15
No WhatsApp, Call, or Book-a-Viewing Action on Car Pages
The only action on a car page is a single "شراء" (Buy) button. In the Saudi market, WhatsApp is the default channel for car enquiries — its absence from the exact page where desire peaks forces interested buyers to hunt for contact details elsewhere or leave. A car page should offer the full ladder: WhatsApp this car, call now, book a private viewing, request more photos. Each missing rung is a measurable leak of ready buyers.
16
No Financing Calculator or Trade-In Path Anywhere
There is no monthly-payment calculator, no bank financing information, and no trade-in valuation journey on the site. For the volume side of the inventory (MG, Elantra, family SUVs) financing is how the majority of Saudi buyers purchase, and "what would this cost monthly?" is the first question. For the premium side, trade-in/consignment of the current car is often the door-opener. Both journeys are absent.
17
Sold Cars Dead-End the Visitor Instead of Redirecting Interest
Sold vehicles remain browsable (the Porsche Targa GTS page carries a "مباع" badge) but offer the visitor no next step — no "similar cars available now," no "notify me when a comparable car arrives," no enquiry option. A visitor who found their way to a sold car has declared exactly what they want; the current page wastes that declaration. This is among the cheapest conversion wins available.
18
No Lead Capture Exists for Visitors Who Aren't Ready Today
There is no mechanism to convert an interested-but-not-ready visitor into a known lead: no stock alerts ("tell me when a GLE arrives"), no price-drop notifications, no saved-cars account with follow-up, no newsletter, no callback request. The site converts only visitors ready to press "Buy" in the moment — the smallest slice of its traffic — and loses the identity of everyone else.
19
On Mobile, the Hero Slider Occupies the Entire First Screen
On a phone — where the majority of Saudi traffic arrives — the first full screen is the slider: a cut-out car, a long descriptive paragraph, and slider dots. No inventory, no search, no action is visible without scrolling. Combined with finding 3 (nine seconds of blank screen), the mobile first impression is a long wait followed by a screen that offers nothing to do.
Search Visibility & Sharing — Findings 20–27
20
The Meta Description Is an Empty String
The homepage ships <meta name="description" content=""> — the tag exists and is empty, and the meta keywords tag is equally blank. The text Google shows under the site's name in search results is the single highest-leverage copy on the web, and it is currently blank. PageSpeed's own SEO audit flags this ("Description text is empty"). Every page on the site shares this condition.
21
No H1 Heading Exists, and the Title Tag Carries Zero Keywords
The homepage has no H1 element at all, and its title tag reads "السبر | الرئيسية" ("Al Saber | Home") — no "cars," no "luxury," no city, no brand names. Google's two strongest on-page relevance signals are the title and H1; the site provides one of them empty of meaning and the other not at all. Searches like "معارض سيارات فاخرة الرياض" have no reason to surface this site.
22
No Sitemap and No robots.txt — Google Crawls Blind
Both /sitemap.xml and /robots.txt return 404 (the robots URL renders an English "Not Found" error page). A sitemap is how a dealer site tells Google "here are my 200 cars, and this one arrived today." Without it, new inventory is discovered only by chance, and there is no crawl guidance whatsoever. For a business whose product rotates weekly, this structurally disconnects the inventory from search.
23
125 of 126 Images Have No Alt Text — the Inventory Is Invisible to Google Images
Effectively every image on the homepage — every car, every brand — carries an empty or missing alt attribute. Google Images is a major discovery channel for car shoppers ("G63 2019 black"), and the entire inventory is excluded from it. The alt gap also contributes to the accessibility failures in finding 28 and wastes the image-heavy nature of the site entirely.
24
Zero Structured Data — No Car Can Appear as a Rich Result
No JSON-LD or microdata exists on any page: no Vehicle, no AutoDealer, no Offer markup. Structured data is what lets Google show a car with its price, year, and mileage directly in results, and what feeds "car dealer near me" surfaces. Competitors with marked-up inventory occupy this space unchallenged.
25
Zero Open Graph Tags — Shared Links Show as Bare URLs
The site has no Open Graph or Twitter Card tags. When anyone shares a car on WhatsApp — the primary way cars circulate socially in the Kingdom — the message shows a naked link with no photo, no car name, no price. Every organic share, the cheapest marketing the dealership can get, arrives stripped of its selling power. For a luxury car business this is arguably the single most costly small gap on the list.
26
Arabic Only — the English-Speaking Luxury Market Cannot Use the Site
The site exists in a single language with no English version and no hreflang configuration. Riyadh and Jeddah host a substantial international and expatriate segment that shops for premium cars in English — searches like "Rolls Royce for sale Riyadh" or "Ferrari dealer Saudi Arabia" cannot land on this site in any meaningful way. Half the addressable luxury market is structurally excluded.
27
The Server Rejects Non-Browser Clients With HTTP 406
Direct HTTP requests without full browser headers receive a 406 "Not Acceptable" response. Standard browsers work, but this configuration can silently block or degrade SEO tools, some crawler user-agents, link-preview bots, and monitoring services. Combined with the missing robots.txt, the site's relationship with automated clients — which is what search engines are — is unmanaged and partially hostile.
Accessibility & Quality — Findings 28–30
28
Accessibility Score 82 — Unnamed Controls, Contrast Failures, Small Touch Targets
PageSpeed's accessibility audit flags buttons without accessible names, links without discernible text, foreground/background contrast failures, touch targets below minimum size, no main landmark, and heading levels out of order. Beyond the users it excludes, these are the mechanics behind mis-taps and hard-to-read text for every mobile user — and several of them overlap with the SEO gaps (unnamed links are unreadable to Google too).
29
Images Served at Low Resolution and Wrong Aspect Ratios
The Best Practices audit flags images displayed at incorrect aspect ratios and images served below the resolution of the screens displaying them. In practice: on a modern phone or retina display, photographs of six-figure cars render slightly stretched or slightly blurred. Paradoxically, the site is both too heavy (34 MB) and too low-quality where it counts — the worst of both trades.
30
Google Has Too Little Traffic Data to Even Assess the Site
The Core Web Vitals field assessment returns "Not applicable" — the site does not receive enough real-user traffic for Google's Chrome UX Report to compile data on it. This is a low-severity finding only because it is a symptom rather than a cause: it independently confirms that the visibility problems documented in findings 20–27 translate into a traffic level below Google's measurement floor.
